Global food system is structurally vulnerable

Food system dependence on standardized industrial package and over-reliance on external inputs incl. fossil fuels makes them even more vulnerable in the current times of geopolitical uncertainty and shift towards a multipolar world.

Over the last decades they managed to successfully feed increasing numbers of global population however, the

Elements of structural fragility

Just-in-time supply chains

No buffer stock. Any disruption — weather, conflict, logistics failure — translates immediately to shelf shortages.

Fossil fuel dependence

Fertilizers, transport, refrigeration, and mechanised farming are all oil-dependent. Price shocks transmit directly into food costs.

Geographic concentration

A small number of regions supply the majority of global staples. Climate stress in one area affects global prices.

Retail oligopoly

A handful of supermarket chains control UK grocery supply. Cost externalisation onto suppliers and consumers is baked in.

Food systems are highly vulnerable to tipping point impacts as they are affected by multiple environmental dimensions, with particular sensitivity to precipitation and temperature.

Agricultural systems are strongly sensitive to changes in the functioning of a wide range of supporting systems in soil, water, pollination and natural pest suppression.

The global food system is also a potential amplifier of tipping point impacts as it sits within a complex set of interacting biophysical and social systems.

Moreover, harvest failures occurring simultaneously in more than one major crop-producing region would pose a major threat to global food security (Kornhuber et al., 2023). Significant reductions in global production are likely to produce wide-ranging social, economic and political disturbance (Gaupp, 2020). No strategic reserve

Defra's Agriculture in the UK 2024 reports the UK's production-to-supply ratio at 65% for all food and 77% for indigenous-type food — up from 62% and 75% the year before, but from a low base and against a long-term drift.

The 2020 wheat crop fell by 40.5% year on year, the largest year-on-year drop in production since records began in 1892; food inflation peaked at 19.1% in March 2023; and Trust for London's food-insecurity indicator jumped 23% in a single year.

There is no formal UK strategic food reserve in the sense that Sweden, Finland, or Switzerland maintain one,

and the government's public advice — announced by the Deputy Prime Minister on 22 May 2024, the same morning the general election was called — that households store three days of food, was, as Tim Lang notes in Just in Case, "context-free… ill thought-through." It was never followed up.

February 2023:

Recent Shocks

rationing tomatoes in a ‘rich country’

In February 2023, Tesco, Asda, Aldi and Morrisons rationed tomatoes, cucumbers, peppers, lettuce and other produce to two or three items per customer. Shelves stood empty for weeks.

The cause was mundane — bad weather in Spain and Morocco, compounded by high energy costs that made UK glasshouse growers switch off — and that is exactly the point.

The UK imports 90–95% of its fresh produce in winter, mostly from those two countries. A cold snap abroad emptied British shelves.

This was not a war or a pandemic. It was a weather event hitting an abnormally fragile system — and it exposed the deeper vulnerability: a population that expects year-round tomatoes has no fallback when the tomatoes stop.

COVID panic-buying, Brexit border friction, and the 2022 Ukraine-driven energy and fertiliser spike are the same lesson in different clothes. The shocks are not hypothetical or future. They are recent, recurring, and getting more frequent.

(1) a coherent national food policy centred on feeding people healthily and sustainably;

(2) narrowing the civil food resilience gaps that run deep through state structures;

(3) resetting production toward public health on a more regional and geographically diverse basis;

Schools & Children

The Camden numbers behind the parcels are worse than the borough average would suggest. Nearly 45% of Camden's schoolchildren are eligible for free school meals — the fourth highest rate in England, behind only Manchester, Islington and Hackney.

Two out of every five Camden children were living in poverty in 2021/22, against a London average of a third; Camden residents spend on average 57% of their income on rent. C2 · WHAT IT DOES FOR A PERSON

A route up and out by more than one door

The system argument only matters because of what it does to a person's life, and that is worth stating plainly.

Moving through the network is not about being promoted off a list — it is a change in what a person gets, how they are treated, and who they become.

The honest caveat keeps the argument credible: the crisis door must stay open.

Someone in acute crisis this week cannot join a co-op their way out of it, and demand for emergency food is rising as the crisis fund shrinks. The aim is not to close doors — it is to make sure there is more than one, and that the doors connect, so that fewer people need the parcel next month because their income stretches further, their underlying problems are being addressed, and they are plugged into a community instead of a queue. That is what "from crisis to community wealth" means in one person's life.

Through the crisis door, you arrive in need and receive a parcel of what you are given; the need recurs, and you are a recipient.

Through the capacity-building door, you pay a little and choose your food, you come regularly, and someone there helps with the debt, benefits or housing problem underneath — so the thing that caused the crisis starts to get fixed.

Through the self-organising door, you are a member who helps run it: buying together lowers your bill week after week, and you leave with neighbours you know, confidence you did not have, and a share of real collective power.

The most useful frame comes from the UK's largest study of community food — Dr Megan Blake's survey of 14,156 low-income households — which distinguishes three functions:

Rung 1, Catching — crisis relief: food banks, for people in immediate crisis;

Rung 2, Capacity-building — affordable food clubs (the membership models also known as pantries, larders and social supermarkets), plus community kitchens and cookery; and

Rung 3, Self-organising — community-led co-ops and enterprises that reshape local food systems.

The "Food Ladder," here is a useful metaphor as it does relates to people's capacity to feed themselves and organize. In practice, however, this is a network of options, not necessarily a ranking to climb.

People move between the rungs as circumstances change, a food bank is exactly the right door in an acute week/month/crisis shock.

Food banks are the infrastructure of acute emergency response; that is not a lower rung of human worth, it is a different job.

What the frame gives us, then, is not a hierarchy but a map of routes — the different doors through which a person, or a whole neighbourhood, can move toward security.

This reframing matters for what the borough is trying to do. The aim is not to empty the crisis function into the co-op function, or to declare one form superior. It is twofold: to help people move up and out of dependence on crisis provision wherever they can — by whichever route fits their life — and to build genuine purchasing power through cooperative procurement, so that the whole system buys better, fresher and cheaper.

Cooperation Town

- self organizing in practice

Cooperation Town runs 12 co-ops in Camden, where members meet weekly to source and share food and make collective savings of roughly £330,000 a year — money that stays in the household budgets of families in low- or no-income work, social housing, and migrant and global-majority communities.

Its Gospel Oak logistics hub processes over 100 tonnes of cooperatively sourced food a year, and their own conclusion from running it is the sentence that connects this whole workshop: "when organising at scale, co-ops have far greater purchasing power."

Cooperation Town describes its aim to work with local authorities to establish the community cooperative model as "a cost-effective alternative or supplementary infrastructure to food banks, based on democratic, member-led economic participation" — an offer of participation, not charity.

Its politics are equally explicit: it organises around household costs as a route to building power in working-class communities, drawing on autonomist and cooperative traditions, and it treats food as the tangible, everyday issue through which people who never saw themselves as organisers gain the confidence to lead.

That has produced far more than savings: co-op members have organised an eviction resistance, started a community laundrette, and run a women's health group, and the Gospel Oak hub has grown into a social hub — a free neighbourhood laundrette, the Gospel Oak Living Room serving cooked food six days a week, a learning garden, and free space for other groups. What is dynamic food procurement?

Often, local authority food purchasing contracts are held by large corporations in the UK, with smaller local food producers being unable to supply these catering contracts because of barriers to entry such as complex paperwork.

Dynamic procurement provides a solution to this by creating a digital platform for food producers and buyers that breaks down large tenders into small lots. This enables contracts to be fulfilled by a mixture of different (including small) suppliers, because local suppliers have flexibility to move in and out of the system depending on availability, compared to conventional framework contracts which tend to limit access due to their stringent pre-qualification requirements.

A series of successful pilots including in Bath and North East Somerset have shown the potential of using dynamic procurement to deliver a more coordinated and responsive procurement process that better reflects the active and immediate nature of the local food supply.

Recognising the cost-effectiveness of these pilots, dynamic procurement has been recommended by the National Food Strategy and Parliament’s Environment, Food and Rural Affairs Committee to be implemented by public bodies across the UK.

Under supply stress, foods sort into fragile and resilient.

Fragile: out-of-season imported fruit and vegetables, foods on long single-source supply chains, and anything dependent on cheap energy and fertiliser.

Resilient: locally growable seasonal vegetables, legumes and pulses, stored grains, root vegetables, and preserved and fermented foods. The resilient plate is not exotic and it is not a dystopia of insects — it is closer to how most of the world, and Britain’s own recent past, has always eaten.

The mispriced plate

Cheap food, in short, buys apparent affordability today by loading un-priced costs onto tomorrow — the opposite of resilience. The first job of “Beyond Price” is to stop pretending the shelf price is the real price.

Globally, the FAO now puts the hidden costs of agrifood systems at $10–12 trillion a year — close to 10% of world GDP — and the striking finding is that over 70% of it comes from diets: unhealthy eating driving disease and lost productivity, with roughly a fifth from environmental damage.

Who pays, who saves (financing prevention)

Here is the structural trap, and it is designed into the system, not a Camden failure: the "wrong-pockets problem" — whoever pays for prevention is rarely whoever banks the saving.

A co-op, Camden Giving and a shrinking crisis fund carry the cost of community food; the savings land in different budgets — the NHS, mental-health services, the DWP. Even where the return is real, no single budget-holder has the incentive to invest, and prevention is starved everywhere.

The UK numbers make the trap concrete. The public health grant (local government's prevention money) has been cut ~28% per person in real terms since 2015/16 and now sits at just 2.2% of the NHS England budget — even though a year of good health bought through local-authority prevention costs about £3,800, three-to-four times cheaper than the ~£13,500 it costs through NHS treatment. And the direction self-reinforces: the Institute for Government documents a prevention "doom-loop" — preventative local-authority spending cut 77.9% (2009/10–2022/23) while acute spending on the same populations rose 58.1%. Cuts to prevention manufacture the acute demand that then crowds out the next round of prevention.

The escape is not exhortation; it is plumbing that moves money across the pockets — with a clear-eyed read of what works:

Outcomes-based commissioning — pay co-ops for measured wellbeing outcomes, not "food distributed" (Government Outcomes Lab, Oxford). Caveat: GO Lab's own verdict is there's no conclusive evidence it beats a good grant — so use it where the outcome is genuinely measurable, not as dogma.

Cross-budget pooling — Public Health + NHS/ICB co-funding community food because it's cheaper than the health costs it prevents (the point where Beyond-Crisis meets Beyond-Price). Caveat: the Better Care Fund shows pooling improves joint working but rarely delivers cashable savings on its own — pooling needs an outcome it's actually paying for.

Social prescribing routed to community food (SROI clusters ~£2–£9 per £1 across UK reviews — directionally positive, but the academic consensus is the economic evidence is still weak; treat as promising, not proven).

A resilience or health bond — capital fronted by an investor, repaid from documented avoided cost. The instrument exists in principle (Health Affairs' Social-Drivers-of-Health bonds explicitly name the wrong-pockets problem it solves) but no operational food-specific health bond exists yet — for Camden this is a precedent to invent, not cite.

Each depends on one unglamorous prerequisite: evidencing the counterfactual precisely enough that a finance director acts on it. That evidence task is itself a portfolio item.

Camden's crisis-and-resilience fund is already shrinking (~£500k). The choice isn't whether to fund prevention — it's whether to let the cut fall or route a share of the avoided cost back to the organisations creating it. A second circuit (new money and value flows)

The levers above optimise the existing system; the next move builds a parallel one — and this is where the brief should be most honest. A local currency designed around food — the lineage runs Bristol Pound → Brixton Pound → a possible Camden Pound — is money engineered to keep food spend circulating locally and reward short, resilient supply chains. The economic rationale is sound (NEF's Plugging the Leaks / local-multiplier work). But both flagship UK currencies wound down: the Bristol Pound (peak ~£1m issued, 800+ businesses, even payable as council tax) closed its digital scheme in 2020 and its successor folded in 2023; the Brixton Pound is effectively paused. Academic evaluations judged them symbolically powerful but too small to move the local economy without perpetual grant funding. The transferable lesson isn't the currency — it's the Brixton Fund (a small levy on transactions pooled into community grants allocated by user vote): couple any local money to a visible local-benefit pot, and design for self-sustainability from day one or don't start.

The more robust build is resident purchasing power itself. Camden's own Cooperation Town (founded here, Kentish Town, 2019; now 50+ co-ops) shows neighbours pooling into bulk orders cut food bills 30–40%; aggregated up through the Hub, that organised household demand can contract directly with regional farms (the Better Food Shed pattern: ~23 farms, ~20 tonnes/week, already supplying London institutions), and a Food Credit Rail / Universal Basic Nutrient Income puts guaranteed purchasing power for good food into residents' hands. Digital rails already exist (Open Food Network UK: 2,200+ producers, £7m+ of local food sales).

Read together these aren't welfare tweaks; they are the beginnings of a second, community-owned route to good food that runs alongside the market and the crisis door — one that keeps working, and keeps value local, precisely when the mainstream system is under price or supply stress. That is demand-side purchasing power turned into supply-side resilience.

Beyond Price

Capitalising the backbone (assets, land and community food as infrastructure)

A parallel system needs a balance sheet, not just a grant cycle. Two card-clusters supply it. First, community food as economic infrastructure: the co-ops, pantries and shared Hub are the demand-aggregation and distribution backbone the second circuit runs on — and, valued properly (BP·2), they are preventative infrastructure with real returns, not discretionary spend. Second, patient capital for the physical assets: a Soil Fund or municipal/community bond that acquires land and finances conversion to regional, agroecological production — capital repaid, like the health bond, from documented value and avoided cost.

The instruments are proven — and one of them, Camden has already run. In 2022 the borough raised £1 million from 398 residents through a Community Municipal Investment (a "Local Climate Bond" via Abundance), paying 1.75% over five years to fund solar, EV charging and greener streets. The appetite and the plumbing exist; the same instrument, re-scoped, could capitalise a food hub, growing sites or shared cold storage — making Camden a potential first-mover on a food-focused municipal bond, since no UK council has yet aimed one at food (state it as a mechanism transfer, not an existing precedent). Alongside it, community shares show residents will put real equity directly into food assets: Fordhall Farm raised £800k from 8,000 £50-shareholders to buy the farm itself; the Ecological Land Co-operative and Kindling Farm bought land for new ecological growers — part of £210m invested by 130,000 people across 540 community businesses through the regulated model. A borough-catalysed community benefit society could acquire peri-urban land or fit out processing space the same way.

There is a real gap to be honest about: the "food is medicine, and it saves money" evidence is strong but overwhelmingly US (medically-tailored meals: ~49% fewer admissions, ~$1.57 saved per $1, ~$13.6bn national). The UK case rests on cost-of-inaction (the £268bn) and SROI (green social prescribing ~£2.4–£5.4 per £1), not on a completed UK avoided-cost trial. That gap is not a reason to wait — it is the argument for a pilot with the counterfactual built in.

This is also where Beyond Price closes the loop with preparedness. The National Preparedness Commission's recommendation — a borough Food Resilience Committee, a community food-asset audit, treating existing community food infrastructure as latent emergency infrastructure — only becomes real if that infrastructure is capitalised and owned, not left to secure its own surplus, funding and attention alone. The economics chapter is what pays for the resilience the rest of the brief describes.
