# C · BEYOND PRICE — New Food Economics
### Situation Room · Chapter draft (v2, research-grounded) · for the 5th/6th workshop

> **The frame.** Food is priced wrong. The harm cheap food does and the value good food creates are both invisible to the price on the shelf — so the market steers Camden's food system by a signal that leaves out most of what matters. A borough that cannot see the true cost of its food cannot fund its own resilience. This chapter makes that value legible and then moves money to match it: from the pocket that saves to the pocket that pays, from national distributors to the regional economy, and from charitable relief to preventative infrastructure. Every move in it is a resilience move — the economics that pays for the preparedness the rest of the brief describes.

*A note on the evidence.* Where a figure is strong and current, it's cited. Where the evidence is thin or a precedent failed, this draft says so plainly — the honest version is the more useful one for the table.

---

## BP·1 — The mispriced plate

The price at the till is a fiction of cheapness. Globally, the FAO now puts the **hidden costs of agrifood systems at $10–12 trillion a year — close to 10% of world GDP** — and the striking finding is that **over 70% of it comes from diets**, not farming: unhealthy eating driving disease and lost productivity, with roughly a fifth from environmental damage. The Rockefeller Foundation ran the same logic on the US and found food costs **about three times its checkout price** once health and environment are counted.

The UK number to hold in the room is now sharper than the old "£1 of food, £1 of hidden cost" (Sustainable Food Trust, 2017 — still true, but dated). In November 2024 the **Food, Farming and Countryside Commission** put the UK food system's **health harms alone at ~£268 billion a year** — nearly the entire NHS budget — split across ~£67.5bn healthcare, £14.3bn social care, £10.1bn welfare, and £116.4bn lost productivity. Their invert of it is the whole chapter in one line: giving **everyone** a government-recommended healthy diet would cost about **£57bn — under a quarter of the damage** the current system does. *"Solving this crisis would cost a fraction of what we currently spend ignoring it."* The disease is already local: **obesity costs the UK ~£98bn+/yr**, **type-2 diabetes ~£14bn**, and **malnutrition ~£19.6bn to the NHS** — most of it diet-driven, most of it preventable.

Cheap food, in short, buys apparent affordability today by loading un-priced costs onto tomorrow — the opposite of resilience. The first job of Beyond Price is to stop pretending the shelf price is the real price.

*Sources.* FAO, *State of Food and Agriculture* 2023/2024 ($10–12tn; >70% diets); Rockefeller Foundation, *True Cost of Food* (2021); FFCC, *The False Economy of Big Food* (Nov 2024, ~£268bn; ~£57bn to fix); Sustainable Food Trust, *Hidden Cost of UK Food* (2017); Frontier Economics/Nesta (obesity); Univ. of York (diabetes ~£14bn, 2024); BAPEN (malnutrition ~£19.6bn).

**Question for the table:** *If Camden's food is costing multiples of its price — in health, welfare and lost productivity — where is that bill landing, and on whose budget?*

## BP·2 — Making the invisible visible *(translating value → price)*

Naming the hidden cost is half the move; the other half is naming the hidden *value*. Community food looks like a cost line — groceries moved, volunteers managed — but most of what it produces never reaches an invoice: confidence, connection, reduced isolation, financial stability, prevention. There is now a **Treasury-endorsed way to price exactly this**. HM Treasury's Green Book supplementary guidance (2021) adopted the **WELLBY** — one point of life-satisfaction, per person, per year, valued at **~£13,000** — and HACT's **UK Social Value Bank** converts it into 88 costed outcomes (e.g. relief from depression/anxiety ~£40,000; sustaining employment ~£14,000; feeling connected to community ~£3,750 per person/year). Applied to the Cooperation Town model in this brief, the shape of the argument is consistent: the largest values community food generates are **social and psychological, not nutritional**.

That is the "Beyond Price" pathway quantified — the strongest case for funding community food as **preventative infrastructure, not charitable relief**. Its mirror image is **Cost-of-Inaction Accounting**: pricing what Camden pays by *not* acting — the A&E visits, crisis grants and referrals a well-supported resident never draws on. Value made visible on one side, avoided cost on the other, together turn a moral argument into a financial one a director of finance can act on. (Handle both honestly: these are orders-of-magnitude proxies, not audited returns — and the table should treat them as such.)

*Sources.* HM Treasury Green Book *Wellbeing* supplementary guidance (2021; WELLBY ~£13,000); HACT UK Social Value Bank v7 (Simetrica-Jacobs); Public Services (Social Value) Act 2012; this brief (Cooperation Town HACT calculation).
*Portfolio:* **Cost-of-Inaction Accounting**; the social-value spine of **Camden Pound & Social-Value procurement**.

**Question for the table:** *What would Camden have to start measuring — which avoided costs, which wellbeing outcomes — for its finance system to treat community food as investment, not spend?*

## BP·3 — Who pays, who saves *(financing prevention)*

Here is the structural trap, and it is designed into the system, not a Camden failure: the **"wrong-pockets problem"** — *whoever pays for prevention is rarely whoever banks the saving.* A co-op, Camden Giving and a shrinking crisis fund carry the cost of community food; the savings land in different budgets — the NHS, mental-health services, the DWP. Even where the return is real, no single budget-holder has the incentive to invest, and prevention is starved everywhere.

The UK numbers make the trap concrete. The **public health grant** (local government's prevention money) has been **cut ~28% per person in real terms since 2015/16** and now sits at just **2.2% of the NHS England budget** — even though a year of good health bought through local-authority prevention costs about **£3,800, three-to-four times cheaper than the ~£13,500** it costs through NHS treatment. And the direction self-reinforces: the Institute for Government documents a prevention **"doom-loop"** — preventative local-authority spending cut **77.9%** (2009/10–2022/23) while acute spending on the same populations *rose* 58.1%. Cuts to prevention manufacture the acute demand that then crowds out the next round of prevention.

The escape is not exhortation; it is **plumbing that moves money across the pockets** — with a clear-eyed read of what works:
- **Outcomes-based commissioning** — pay co-ops for measured wellbeing outcomes, not "food distributed" (Government Outcomes Lab, Oxford). *Caveat: GO Lab's own verdict is there's no conclusive evidence it beats a good grant — so use it where the outcome is genuinely measurable, not as dogma.*
- **Cross-budget pooling** — Public Health + NHS/ICB co-funding community food because it's cheaper than the health costs it prevents (the point where Beyond-Crisis meets Beyond-Price). *Caveat: the Better Care Fund shows pooling improves joint working but rarely delivers cashable savings on its own — pooling needs an outcome it's actually paying for.*
- **Social prescribing** routed to community food (SROI clusters ~£2–£9 per £1 across UK reviews — directionally positive, but the academic consensus is the economic evidence is still weak; treat as promising, not proven).
- **A resilience or health bond** — capital fronted by an investor, repaid from documented avoided cost. The instrument exists in principle (Health Affairs' Social-Drivers-of-Health bonds explicitly name the wrong-pockets problem it solves) but **no operational food-specific health bond exists yet** — for Camden this is a precedent to *invent*, not cite.

Each depends on one unglamorous prerequisite: **evidencing the counterfactual** precisely enough that a finance director acts on it. That evidence task is itself a portfolio item.

*Sources.* Urban Institute / *Health Affairs* (wrong-pockets); The Health Foundation, *Options for/Investing in the public health grant* (28% cut; 2.2% of NHS; £3,800 vs £13,500); Institute for Government, *A preventative approach to public services* (2024; 77.9% vs 58.1%); Government Outcomes Lab (outcomes contracts; Life Chances Fund); NAO (Better Care Fund); *Health Affairs* (SDH bonds, 2022); NASP / UCL (social-prescribing SROI, with caveats); Camden Food Partnership notes, 24 Mar 2026 (~£500k crisis-fund cut).
*Portfolio:* **NHS Preventive Food Investment**, **Health as Investment — Nutrition Impact Finance**, **Municipal Bonds**.

**Question for the table:** *Camden's crisis-and-resilience fund is already shrinking (~£500k). The choice isn't whether to fund prevention — it's whether to let the cut fall or route a share of the avoided cost back to the organisations creating it. Which single instrument does Camden pilot first, and what counterfactual would it have to evidence?*

## BP·4 — The money you already move *(public spend as a lever)*

Before Camden raises a new pound, look at the pounds already flowing through its own signature. **~£6.7m a year of school-meal spend** — rising as secondary free school meals expand from September 2026 — is one of the most immediate levers a borough holds: guaranteed, predictable demand is exactly the stable order-book that lets small regional farms invest, convert to sustainable methods and survive. Spent as usual it props up national distributors; spent deliberately it rebuilds the regional food economy around London. As one framing of the institutional-demand literature puts it: without guaranteed markets, most farmers *can't afford* the long-term investment institutional buyers need.

The evidence that local spend compounds is strong. **Community wealth building** — the Preston Model, with CLES — redirected anchor-institution procurement and lifted the share of spend retained **in Preston from 5% to 18.2% (£38m → £111m)** and across Lancashire from 39% to 79.2% (£292m → £486m) in five years, alongside falling unemployment and thousands more workers on the real living wage. Short-chain food spend carries a strong local multiplier: NEF's like-for-like study found **£10 spent with a local box scheme was worth ~£25 to the local economy vs ~£14 at a supermarket** (≈£2.50 vs £1.40 per £1); Hackney's **Growing Communities** — the closest inner-London model — returns **~£3.70 of social, economic and environmental value per £1**; and the Food for Life school-meals SROI is **~£3 per £1** (£3.04 Plymouth, £3.11 Nottinghamshire, NEF 2011). Separately, **£1 invested in cutting food waste returns ~£14** (WRAP). *(These measure different things — a local-vs-supermarket multiplier, a total-value SROI, a waste return — so cite them as distinct, not one "3× rule.")*

The vehicles exist. The **London Food Purchasing Commitment** (ReLondon / Hackney / Sustain, 2023 — Newham a founding signatory) unifies council catering targets for climate-friendly, less-wasted, local-and-sustainable food; the current national ambition is **50% of public-sector food spend on local or higher-standard produce** (Defra, 2024). **Dynamic Food Procurement** is the plumbing that lets SMEs and small farms bid at all — the Bath & NE Somerset pilot cut **food miles 59% and overall spend ~6%** while raising local sourcing. And two cities prove it scales without extra money: **Copenhagen's House of Food reached ~90% organic public meals by 2015 with no budget increase** (via training, seasonality and waste reduction), and **Ghent** embeds a legally-robust *"short-supply-chain"* criterion in its catering tenders. Newham shows the discipline works even without owning the kitchen — attaching Food-for-Life and Purchasing-Commitment conditions to its meal grants.

This is where economics becomes resilience directly: **using demand to change what gets grown, not just what gets served** shortens chains, builds regional relationships, and converts Camden's biggest food budget into supply-side resilience.

*Sources.* CLES / Preston City Council (5%→18.2%, £38m→£111m); NEF, *Plugging the Leaks* (£25 vs £14 local food multiplier, 2002) & *Farmer-focused routes to market: Growing Communities* (£3.70/£1, 2021); NEF (Food for Life SROI £3.04–£3.11, 2011); WRAP (£1→£14 waste); London Food Purchasing Commitment (ReLondon/Sustain, 2023); Defra public-sector food consultation (2024; 50% target); Dynamic Food Procurement — B&NES pilot (−59% food miles, −6% spend); Copenhagen House of Food; Ghent *Gent en Garde*; Contracts Finder (Camden school-meals ~£6.7m/yr); Newham / Juniper (grant-condition lever); *Lancet Planetary Health* (2021), Rockefeller Foundation (guaranteed-demand-enables-investment).
*Portfolio:* **Camden Pound & Social-Value mission-oriented procurement**, **Partner adoption of Camden's Food Buying Standard**, **Healthier Catering Commitment**.

**Question for the table:** *If Camden committed its £6.7m school spend (and the wider plate — care homes, civic buildings) to a local-and-sustainable floor via a Dynamic Purchasing System, what regional supply could that guaranteed demand actually build — and who convenes the farms?*

## BP·5 — Planning gain and the wage floor *(the levers hiding in plain sight)*

Two more levers sit inside the council's own machinery, unused for food. The first is **land-value capture**: developer contributions through **S106 and CIL** are routinely spent on transport, open space and affordable housing — and could, with a policy decision, fund food infrastructure (growing space, community kitchens, a hub). Making **food security a material consideration in planning** would turn it from a voluntary add-on into a requirement major developments must answer to. The second is the **wage floor**: because most Camden food insecurity is an income problem before it is a food problem, procuring and paying for the **London Living Wage** and building food-sector routes into stable work is food policy conducted through the economy, not the food bank — the "Money-First" approach Camden's own action plan already names.

These are the least glamorous cards in the pathway and among the most powerful: they change the economics of food without a single new grant, by re-pointing money and rules the borough already controls.

*Sources.* Camden Food Mission Action Plan 2026 (Money-First / financial-wellbeing; Living Wage work); Living Wage Foundation; planning-gain mechanisms (S106/CIL; material considerations).
*Portfolio:* **Developer Contributions for Food — S106 / CIL Redirection**, **Food Security as a Material Consideration**, **Good Work incl. Living Wage Action group**, **Financial Wellbeing / Neighbourhood Advice**.

**Question for the table:** *Which existing lever — planning gain, the wage floor, procurement — is fastest to re-point at food, and who owns the decision?*

## BP·6 — A second circuit *(new money and value flows)*

The levers above optimise the existing system; the next move builds a parallel one — and this is where the brief should be most honest. A **local currency designed around food** — the lineage runs Bristol Pound → Brixton Pound → a possible **Camden Pound** — is money engineered to keep food spend circulating locally and reward short, resilient supply chains. The economic rationale is sound (NEF's *Plugging the Leaks* / local-multiplier work). **But both flagship UK currencies wound down**: the Bristol Pound (peak ~£1m issued, 800+ businesses, even payable as council tax) closed its digital scheme in 2020 and its successor folded in 2023; the Brixton Pound is effectively paused. Academic evaluations judged them *symbolically powerful but too small to move the local economy* without perpetual grant funding. The transferable lesson isn't the currency — it's the **Brixton Fund** (a small levy on transactions pooled into community grants allocated by user vote): couple any local money to a **visible local-benefit pot**, and design for self-sustainability from day one or don't start.

The more robust build is **resident purchasing power** itself. Camden's own **Cooperation Town** (founded here, Kentish Town, 2019; now 50+ co-ops) shows neighbours pooling into bulk orders cut food bills **30–40%**; aggregated up through the Hub, that organised household demand can contract directly with regional farms (the **Better Food Shed** pattern: ~23 farms, ~20 tonnes/week, already supplying London institutions), and a **Food Credit Rail / Universal Basic Nutrient Income** puts guaranteed purchasing power for good food into residents' hands. Digital rails already exist (Open Food Network UK: 2,200+ producers, £7m+ of local food sales).

Read together these aren't welfare tweaks; they are the beginnings of a **second, community-owned route to good food** that runs alongside the market and the crisis door — one that keeps working, and keeps value local, precisely when the mainstream system is under price or supply stress. That is demand-side purchasing power turned into supply-side resilience.

*Sources.* NEF, *Plugging the Leaks* / LM3; Bristol Pound & Brixton Pound (wind-down; Marshall & O'Neill 2018; Petz & Finch 2025 — "too small to effect significant change"); Cooperation Town (Camden, 2019; 30–40% savings; 50+ co-ops); Better Food Shed / Growing Communities (~23 farms, ~20 t/wk); Open Food Network UK; DML, *Negative Portfolio v2.0* (household pledge & collective-buying instruments).
*Portfolio:* **Local Currencies for Food** *(treat as awareness/local-benefit tool, not a macro lever)*, **UBNI / Food Credit Rail**, and the collective-purchasing spine of the Food Buying Network.

**Question for the table:** *What is the smallest credible "second circuit" for Camden — a food-denominated local pound tied to a community fund, or a collective-buying club at Hub scale — that residents themselves would own, and that could survive without a grant?*

## BP·7 — Capitalising the backbone *(assets, land and community food as infrastructure)*

A parallel system needs a balance sheet, not just a grant cycle. Two card-clusters supply it. First, **community food as economic infrastructure**: the co-ops, pantries and shared Hub are the demand-aggregation and distribution backbone the second circuit runs on — and, valued properly (BP·2), they are preventative infrastructure with real returns, not discretionary spend. Second, **patient capital for the physical assets**: a **Soil Fund** or **municipal/community bond** that acquires land and finances conversion to regional, agroecological production — capital repaid, like the health bond, from documented value and avoided cost.

The instruments are proven — and one of them, Camden has already run. In 2022 the borough raised **£1 million from 398 residents** through a **Community Municipal Investment** (a "Local Climate Bond" via Abundance), paying 1.75% over five years to fund solar, EV charging and greener streets. The appetite and the plumbing exist; the same instrument, re-scoped, could capitalise a **food** hub, growing sites or shared cold storage — making Camden a potential *first-mover on a food-focused municipal bond*, since no UK council has yet aimed one at food (state it as a mechanism transfer, not an existing precedent). Alongside it, **community shares** show residents will put real equity directly into food assets: Fordhall Farm raised **£800k from 8,000 £50-shareholders to buy the farm itself**; the Ecological Land Co-operative and Kindling Farm bought land for new ecological growers — part of **£210m invested by 130,000 people across 540 community businesses** through the regulated model. A borough-catalysed community benefit society could acquire peri-urban land or fit out processing space the same way.

There is a real gap to be honest about: the "food is medicine, and it saves money" evidence is strong but overwhelmingly **US** (medically-tailored meals: ~49% fewer admissions, ~$1.57 saved per $1, ~$13.6bn national). The UK case rests on **cost-of-inaction** (the £268bn) and **SROI** (green social prescribing ~£2.4–£5.4 per £1), not on a completed UK avoided-cost trial. That gap is not a reason to wait — it is the argument for a **pilot with the counterfactual built in**.

This is also where Beyond Price closes the loop with preparedness. The National Preparedness Commission's recommendation — a borough **Food Resilience Committee**, a community food-asset audit, treating existing community food infrastructure as **latent emergency infrastructure** — only becomes real if that infrastructure is *capitalised and owned*, not left to secure its own surplus, funding and attention alone. The economics chapter is what pays for the resilience the rest of the brief describes.

*Sources.* Tufts Food is Medicine Institute / *Health Affairs* / *Nature Medicine* (US MTM ROI); FFCC (£268bn cost of inaction); green social prescribing SROI (MDPI, 2023); *Health Affairs* (SDH bonds; food-specific bond not yet operational); Camden Council / Abundance Investment (Camden Climate Investment, £1m / 398 residents, 2022); Co-operatives UK, *Community Shares Report 2023* (£210m / 130,000 investors / 540 businesses); Fordhall CLI (£800k / 8,000 shareholders); Ecological Land Co-op; Kindling Farm; National Preparedness Commission / Tim Lang, *Just in Case* (2025); HACT Social Value Bank; this brief (Hub as shared plumbing; Soil Fund).
*Portfolio:* **Soil Fund**, **Municipal Bonds for Land Conversion**, **Food as Welfare / Social Assistance Integration**, **Challenge Prize**.

**Question for the table:** *What would Camden have to own — which land, which hub, which capitalised assets — for its community food network to be both a balance-sheet asset and a shock-response layer, rather than a permanently precarious cost?*

## BP·8 — The portfolio, and the hand to the table

Beyond Price is one argument told in one arc:

> **value is invisible → make it visible → move the money from who-saves to who-pays → re-point the levers you already control → build a second circuit → capitalise the backbone.**

Underneath it sits a portfolio of **~22 options** — from Cost-of-Inaction Accounting and NHS Preventive Food Investment, through the Camden Pound, S106 redirection and a local-sustainable procurement floor, to Local Currencies, the Food Credit Rail, the Soil Fund and Municipal Bonds. They are not a plan to adopt wholesale; they are a **menu of moves**, each a way of changing the economics of Camden's food so the borough can afford to be resilient. Some are ready now (procurement, planning gain, the wage floor), some are pilots-with-a-counterfactual (a health/resilience bond, outcomes commissioning), and some are cautionary (a local currency, unless designed to survive) — and saying which is which is part of the work.

That is what the workshop is for. Across the two days the three pathways — **Beyond Crisis · Beyond Camden · Beyond Price** — go to **six groups** to be pressure-tested: which moves are real, which are ready, which Camden owns the levers for, and which the room would put its weight behind. Beyond Price brings the hardest and most enabling question of the three — *not what good food is worth, but who pays for it, who saves, and what moves the money between them.*

*Framing throughout:* resilience and preparedness. Every instrument here exists to let Camden fund the prevention and shock-readiness that cheap food, priced wrong, will never pay for by itself — the just-in-case economics for a just-in-time system that has already, in February 2023, run out of tomatoes.
